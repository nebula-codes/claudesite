Optical Illusions Lab

Your Brain is Lying to You

Explore the fascinating ways your brain interprets (and misinterprets) visual information. Each illusion reveals something profound about human perception.

Hermann Grid Illusion

What You See:

Gray dots appear at the intersections of the white lines, but they vanish when you look directly at them.

Why It Happens:

Lateral inhibition in your retina. Cells that detect brightness inhibit neighboring cells. At intersections, the inhibition is stronger from all sides, creating the phantom dark spots.

Rotating Snakes

What You See:

The circles appear to rotate even though this is a completely static image. The effect is stronger in peripheral vision.

Why It Happens:

Microsaccades (tiny eye movements) combined with the specific sequence of light-to-dark transitions trick your motion detection neurons into firing.

Checker Shadow Illusion

What You See:

Squares A and B appear to be different shades - one light, one dark.

The Truth:

They are EXACTLY the same color! Click "Reveal Truth" to see the proof.

Why It Happens:

Your brain adjusts for lighting and shadows. It "knows" B is in shadow, so it interprets it as a lighter color than it actually is. Context matters more than raw data.

Café Wall Illusion

What You See:

The horizontal lines appear to be slanted or curved.

The Truth:

All horizontal lines are perfectly parallel! The offset black and white tiles create the illusion.

Why It Happens:

The contrast between black and white tiles at different offsets confuses edge-detection neurons, creating false perception of tilt.

Ebbinghaus Illusion

What You See:

The orange circle on the left appears larger than the one on the right.

The Truth:

Both orange circles are EXACTLY the same size!

Why It Happens:

Relative size perception. Your brain compares each circle to its surroundings. Surrounded by large circles, it looks small. Surrounded by small circles, it looks large.

Motion Aftereffect

How to Experience It:

1) Click "Start" and stare at the CENTER of the spiral for 30 seconds.
2) Don't look away! Keep your eyes fixed on the center.
3) After 30 seconds, look at something still (like text on this page).

What You'll See:

The text or still objects will appear to move in the opposite direction!

Why It Happens:

Motion-detecting neurons become fatigued from continuous stimulation. When you look away, neurons detecting opposite motion are relatively more active, creating phantom motion.

Impossible Triangle

What You See:

A three-dimensional triangle that seems to exist... but can't.

Why It's Impossible:

Each corner appears locally correct, but the object as a whole cannot exist in 3D space. It's a 2D drawing that tricks your brain's 3D interpretation systems.

The Science:

Your brain makes assumptions about 3D structure from 2D images. This illusion exploits those assumptions by presenting impossible geometry that seems plausible at first glance.

Color Constancy Illusion

What You See:

The strawberries look red, even though there's no red pixels in the image!

Why It Happens:

Color constancy - your brain adjusts colors based on lighting context. It "knows" strawberries should be red, so it perceives them as red despite the cyan filter.

The Science of Perception

Your Brain Predicts Reality

You don't see reality directly. Your brain constructs a model based on expectations, past experiences, and incomplete sensory data. Illusions reveal where these predictions go wrong.

Processing Shortcuts

Your visual system processes 10 million bits of information per second. To handle this, it takes shortcuts and makes assumptions. Most of the time these work perfectly. Illusions exploit the edge cases.

Context is Everything

Your brain doesn't analyze objects in isolation. Surrounding colors, lighting, motion, and even your expectations all influence what you perceive.

Adaptation and Fatigue

Neurons that fire continuously become fatigued. This creates aftereffects like the motion illusion. It's the same principle behind seeing spots after a camera flash.
